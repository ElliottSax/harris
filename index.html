About Martin Harris

A Complex, Honest Seeker

Martin Harris (1783-1875) was a prosperous farmer, treasure seeker, and one of the Three Witnesses to the Book of Mormon—a man whose honest seeking and willingness to speak his truth makes his story endlessly fascinating.

Born in Easton, New York, Harris was shaped by the religious fervor and folk magic traditions of his time. Like many in the "burned-over district," he participated in treasure-seeking activities using seer stones, a practice that later intersected with his involvement in Joseph Smith's translation work.

His journey was anything but straightforward. Harris financed the Book of Mormon's publication at great personal cost, testified to seeing the golden plates and an angel, yet was excommunicated in 1837. He explored various religious movements—joining the Strangites, Whitmerites, and others—always searching, always honest about what he experienced and when he disagreed. His various accounts of his witness experience reveal a man grappling with spiritual versus physical sight, never denying his core experience but honest about its nature.

What makes Harris remarkable isn't unwavering certainty, but his persistent honesty and spiritual seeking—qualities that invite us to explore restoration history with the same openness he brought to his own spiritual journey.

A Life of Seeking

1783

Birth in Easton, New York

Martin Harris was born on May 18, 1783, in Easton, New York, to Nathan and Rhoda Harris, into a world alive with religious revival and folk magic.

1808

Marriage and Prosperity

Married Lucy Harris and established himself as one of Palmyra's most prosperous farmers, accumulating substantial property and wealth.

1820s

Treasure Seeking and Folk Magic

Participated in treasure-seeking activities common to the region, using seer stones and divining rods—practices that bridged religious seeking and folk tradition.

1827

Encounters Joseph Smith

Meets Joseph Smith and becomes fascinated by his account of golden plates. Their shared background in treasure seeking creates common ground.

1828

The Lost 116 Pages

Serves as scribe for the translation. Takes 116 pages home to convince his skeptical wife Lucy, but they are lost—never to be recovered. This event strains his marriage and tests his commitment.

1829

The Witness Experience

Becomes one of the Three Witnesses. His testimony would later be described in various ways—sometimes emphasizing the spiritual nature of the vision, at other times affirming its physical reality. This complexity reflects his honest grappling with sacred experience.

1830

Mortgages Everything

Mortgages his farm for $3,000 (equivalent to over $90,000 today) to finance the Book of Mormon's first printing. This act of faith would lead to years of financial hardship.

1831

Marriage Ends

His wife Lucy leaves him, partly due to his religious involvement and financial losses. She later testifies against the Book of Mormon in public meetings.

1837

Excommunication

Excommunicated from the Church during the Kirtland financial crisis. Publicly criticizes Joseph Smith's financial dealings and leadership. His honesty about perceived wrongs costs him his standing.

1846

Joins the Strangites

After Joseph Smith's death, joins James Strang's movement, attracted by Strang's claim to have received plates and divine revelation. Always seeking authentic spiritual experience.

1847-1855

Religious Wandering

Explores various restoration movements including the Whitmerites, the Gladden Bishop group, and others. Testifies to his Book of Mormon witness at revival meetings while affiliated with different churches.

1855

The Shakers

Briefly affiliated with the Shakers, sharing his witness testimony with them and expressing openness to their spiritual experiences—demonstrating his ecumenical approach to religious truth.

1869

Spiritual Eyes vs. Natural Eyes

In an interview, makes his famous distinction about seeing the plates with "spiritual eyes" rather than natural vision—an honest clarification that sparked controversy but reflected his commitment to truthfulness.

1870

Journey to Utah

At age 87, accepts an invitation to move to Utah, his way paid by faithful Latter-day Saints. Reunites with the Utah branch of the restoration movement.

1875

Death in Clarkston

Dies in Clarkston, Utah, on July 10, 1875. To the end, he maintained his witness while remaining honest about the nature of his spiritual experiences—never denying, always clarifying.

The Evolving Testimony

Martin Harris's witness testimony evolved and clarified over time—not through denial, but through honest refinement. His various statements reveal a man committed to truth-telling, even when it complicated his narrative.

The Printed Testimony (1830)

"We...have seen and hefted...the plates...And we also know that they have been translated by the gift and power of God...we declare with words of soberness, that an angel of God came down from heaven, and he brought and laid before our eyes, that we beheld and saw the plates."

"Spiritual Eyes" (1838-1869)

When pressed by skeptics, Harris clarified he saw the plates "with the eye of faith" or "spiritual eyes"—not denying his experience, but honestly distinguishing it from ordinary physical sight. This nuance troubled some but demonstrated his integrity.

The Table Analogy

Harris reportedly compared his vision to seeing a city through a mountain: "If you were to ask me if I saw it with my natural eyes, I would say no. Yet I saw it as distinctly as I see anything around me—though at the time they were covered with a cloth."

Never Denied

Despite excommunication, poverty, and changing religious affiliations, Harris never recanted his core witness. He testified to Strangites, Shakers, and eventually Utah Mormons—always maintaining something profound happened in 1829.

Historical Value

His evolving testimonies provide rich material for historians. Rather than simple proof, they offer insight into 19th-century religious experience, the nature of spiritual witness, and one man's lifelong grappling with the sacred.

Honesty as Legacy

Perhaps Harris's greatest gift is his refusal to simplify. He showed it's possible to maintain conviction while acknowledging complexity—a model for honest historical and spiritual inquiry.

The Whimsical & The Sacred

Martin Harris saw the world through eyes attuned to wonder. His life is full of stories that reveal his spiritual sensitivity, his eccentricity, and his willingness to find the divine in unexpected places.

🦌 The Deer and the Christ

In one of the most endearing stories about Harris, he reportedly encountered a deer in the woods and experienced what he understood as a mystical encounter with Christ. This blend of nature mysticism and Christian devotion was characteristic of his spiritual openness—seeing the divine not only in visions of angels but in the created world itself. The story embodies his whimsical yet earnest approach to religious experience.

💰 The Money Digger

Before the Book of Mormon, Harris participated in treasure-seeking expeditions with seer stones and divining rods. Far from being embarrassed by this folk magic past, he saw it as part of his spiritual journey. His openness about these experiences—even after joining respectable society—shows his refreshing lack of pretension about his path to faith.

🎤 The Traveling Testifier

Even while affiliated with competing restoration movements, Harris traveled giving his Book of Mormon testimony. Imagine: a Strangite testifying to Joseph Smith's plates, or telling Shakers about his witness. He refused to let institutional boundaries limit his sharing of what he'd experienced—a true ecumenical spirit.

📜 The Lost Manuscript

The loss of the 116 pages haunted Harris for years. Some saw it as his great failure; he may have seen it as his test. The incident shows his vulnerability to pressure (from his wife Lucy), his willingness to take risks, and the real human cost of being caught between family and faith. His honesty about this failure makes him more relatable than many religious figures.

🏡 The Generous Friend

Despite losing his farm to publish the Book of Mormon, Harris remained generous. He helped other believers financially when he could, shared his testimony freely, and welcomed spiritual seekers. His financial sacrifice wasn't a one-time dramatic gesture—it set a pattern for a lifetime of generous seeking and sharing.

👴 The Ancient Traveler

At 87 years old, Harris undertook the arduous journey to Utah—not because he needed convincing, but because his seeking spirit never rested. Even in old age, he remained curious, open, and willing to journey toward new understanding. He died among the Utah Saints, still testifying, still seeking, still honest.
