About Martin Harris

A Complex, Honest Seeker

Martin Harris (1783-1875) was a prosperous farmer, treasure seeker, and one of the Three Witnesses to the Book of Mormon. He spent his life searching for religious truth, moving between different restoration movements as he tried to make sense of his spiritual experiences.

Born in Easton, New York, Harris grew up in the "burned-over district" where religious enthusiasm and folk magic traditions overlapped. He participated in treasure-seeking with seer stones—a common practice in his community that would later connect him to Joseph Smith's work.

Harris mortgaged his farm to publish the Book of Mormon in 1830, but his relationship with the early church was complicated. Excommunicated in 1837 after criticizing Joseph Smith's financial leadership, he spent decades affiliated with various restoration movements: the Strangites, Whitmerites, Gladden Bishop's group, and even the Shakers. He carried his testimony about seeing the golden plates to each community, while also being candid about experiencing the vision with "spiritual eyes" rather than natural sight.

His restless church membership and honest admissions about the nature of his witness make him a perfect patron for studying restoration history. Like Harris, we're interested in the actual human experiences behind these movements—the complexity, the disagreements, the ongoing search for meaning.

A Life of Seeking

1783

Birth in Easton, New York

Martin Harris was born on May 18, 1783, in Easton, New York, to Nathan and Rhoda Harris, into a world alive with religious revival and folk magic.

1808

Marriage and Prosperity

Married Lucy Harris and established himself as one of Palmyra's most prosperous farmers, accumulating substantial property and wealth.

1820s

Treasure Seeking and Folk Magic

Participated in treasure-seeking activities common to the region, using seer stones and divining rods—practices that bridged religious seeking and folk tradition.

1827

Encounters Joseph Smith

Meets Joseph Smith and becomes fascinated by his account of golden plates. Their shared background in treasure seeking creates common ground.

1828

The Lost 116 Pages

Serves as scribe for the translation. Takes 116 pages home to convince his skeptical wife Lucy, but they are lost—never to be recovered. This event strains his marriage and tests his commitment.

1829

The Witness Experience

Becomes one of the Three Witnesses. His testimony would later be described in various ways—sometimes emphasizing the spiritual nature of the vision, at other times affirming its physical reality. This complexity reflects his honest grappling with sacred experience.

1830

Mortgages Everything

Mortgages his farm for $3,000 (equivalent to over $90,000 today) to finance the Book of Mormon's first printing. This act of faith would lead to years of financial hardship.

1831

Marriage Ends

His wife Lucy leaves him, partly due to his religious involvement and financial losses. She later testifies against the Book of Mormon in public meetings.

1837

Excommunication

Excommunicated from the Church during the Kirtland financial crisis. Publicly criticizes Joseph Smith's financial dealings and leadership. His honesty about perceived wrongs costs him his standing.

1846

Joins the Strangites

After Joseph Smith died, Harris joined James Strang's movement. Strang claimed to have received plates and revelations too, which probably appealed to Harris's sense of what the restoration should look like.

1847-1855

Religious Wandering

Explores various restoration movements including the Whitmerites, the Gladden Bishop group, and others. Testifies to his Book of Mormon witness at revival meetings while affiliated with different churches.

1855

The Shakers

Harris even spent time with the Shakers, sharing his Book of Mormon testimony with them while being open to their spiritual gifts. He didn't seem to think you had to pick just one restoration movement.

1869

Spiritual Eyes vs. Natural Eyes

In an interview, makes his famous distinction about seeing the plates with "spiritual eyes" rather than natural vision—an honest clarification that sparked controversy but reflected his commitment to truthfulness.

1870

Journey to Utah

At 87, Harris accepted an invitation from Utah Mormons who paid his travel costs. He spent his final years with the Utah church, one of many restoration groups he'd been part of over his long life.

1875

Death in Clarkston

Died in Clarkston, Utah, on July 10, 1875, at age 92. He was still talking about his witness experience and still making distinctions about spiritual versus physical sight.

The Evolving Testimony

Harris gave different accounts of his witness experience over his lifetime. Sometimes he emphasized the spiritual nature of the vision, other times he affirmed its physical reality. His willingness to clarify and nuance his testimony—even when it created problems for him—tells us something important about how people experience and describe sacred events.

The Printed Testimony (1830)

"We...have seen and hefted...the plates...And we also know that they have been translated by the gift and power of God...we declare with words of soberness, that an angel of God came down from heaven, and he brought and laid before our eyes, that we beheld and saw the plates."

"Spiritual Eyes" (1838-1869)

When skeptics pushed him, Harris would clarify that he saw the plates "with the eye of faith" or "spiritual eyes"—not regular physical sight. This distinction troubled some people who wanted a more straightforward testimony, but Harris stuck to what he actually experienced.

The Table Analogy

Harris reportedly compared his vision to seeing a city through a mountain: "If you were to ask me if I saw it with my natural eyes, I would say no. Yet I saw it as distinctly as I see anything around me—though at the time they were covered with a cloth."

A Testimony Across Churches

Harris carried his witness testimony to every religious community he joined—Strangites, Shakers, Whitmerites, and eventually the Utah church. He kept testifying about what he'd experienced while also being honest about the nature of that experience. His story traveled with him.

Historical Value

For historians, Harris's multiple accounts are actually more useful than a single consistent story would be. They show us how people in the 1800s understood spiritual experiences, how they talked about sacred events, and how those descriptions changed depending on the audience.

Why We Study Him

Harris wouldn't simplify his story even when it would have been easier. He maintained his convictions while admitting to complexity. That's exactly the kind of honesty we want to bring to restoration history—looking at what actually happened, not what we wish had happened.

The Whimsical & The Sacred

Harris saw sacred meaning everywhere—not just in angels and golden plates, but in everyday encounters. These stories show someone who took his spiritual life seriously while also being, well, a bit odd. That combination makes him fascinating.

🦌 The Deer and the Christ

Harris once encountered a deer in the woods and experienced it as a meeting with Christ. To modern ears this sounds strange, but it tells us something about how he understood sacred encounters—they could happen anywhere, not just in formally religious settings. The deer, the golden plates, the angel: all part of a world where the divine and the ordinary overlapped.

💰 The Money Digger

Before the Book of Mormon, Harris went on treasure-seeking expeditions with seer stones and divining rods. He never tried to hide this folk magic background, even after becoming financially successful. That openness helps us understand the cultural context of early Mormon revelations—these practices weren't considered odd in rural New York at the time.

🎤 The Traveling Testifier

Harris kept traveling and testifying about the golden plates even while belonging to churches that competed with the Utah Mormons. Picture it: a Strangite talking about Joseph Smith's plates, or a Shaker sympathizer bearing witness to the Book of Mormon. He clearly didn't think church membership should limit what he shared about his experiences.

📜 The Lost Manuscript

Harris took the first 116 pages of translation home to show his skeptical wife Lucy, and they disappeared. Some people saw it as his great failure. The incident shows how he got caught between family pressure and religious commitment—a very human problem. He never tried to dodge responsibility for it.

🏡 The Generous Friend

Even after losing his farm to publish the Book of Mormon, Harris continued helping other believers financially when he could. He shared his testimony freely and welcomed spiritual seekers. The mortgage wasn't just one dramatic moment—it was part of how he lived his whole life.

👴 The Ancient Traveler

At 87, Harris made the long trip to Utah. Not because he'd decided the Utah church was the only right one, but because he was still seeking, still curious about different restoration movements. He died there a few years later, still talking about his witness experiences.

About the Association

The Martin Harris Historical Association studies the restoration movement as a whole—all the churches, all the splinter groups, all the competing visions of what the restoration should be. We named ourselves after Martin Harris because his life embodies our approach: honest inquiry, willingness to disagree, openness to complexity, and respect for people's actual experiences.

We accept scholarly articles, historical research, primary source analyses, and other contributions for publication. If you're working on restoration history from any tradition or perspective, we'd like to hear from you.
